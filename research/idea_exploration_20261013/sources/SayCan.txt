0010: Do As I Can, Not As I Say:
0011: Grounding Language in Robotic Affordances
0012: Abstract
0013: Large language models can encode a wealth of semantic knowledge about the world. Such knowledge could be extremely useful to robots aiming to act upon high-level, temporally extended instructions expressed in natural language.
0014: However, a significant weakness of language models is that they lack real-world experience, which makes it difficult to leverage them for decision making within a given embodiment.
0015: For example, asking a language model to describe how to clean a spill might result in a reasonable narrative, but it may not be applicable to a particular agent, such as a robot, that needs to perform this task in a particular environment.
0016: We propose to provide real-world grounding by means of pretrained skills, which are used to constrain the model to propose natural language actions that are both feasible and contextually appropriate.
0017: The robot can act as the language model’s “hands and eyes,” while the language model supplies high-level semantic knowledge about the task.
0018: We show how low-level skills can be combined with large language models so that the language model provides high-level knowledge about the procedures for performing complex and temporally extended instructions, while value functions associated with these skills provide the grounding necessary to connect this knowledge to a particular physical environment.
0019: We evaluate our method on a number of real-world robotic tasks, where we show the need for real-world grounding and that this approach is capable of completing long-horizon, abstract, natural language instructions on a mobile manipulator.

0169: 1
0170: Introduction
0171: Recent progress in training large language models (LLMs) has led to systems that can generate long text based on prompts, answer questions, or even engage in a dialog on a wide range of topics. To do so, these models absorb vast quantities of knowledge from text corpora mined from the web.
0172: It is natural to consider whether the knowledge of everyday tasks that is encoded in such models can be used to broaden the set of tasks robots are able to plan and execute, e.g., enabling a robot to perform abstract, temporally-extended textual instructions.
0173: This bears the question: how can embodied agents extract and harness the knowledge of LLMs for physically-grounded tasks?
0174: This question poses a major challenge – LLMs are inherently not grounded in the physical world.
0175: The nature of the training process means that an LLM has never interacted with its environment, has never observed the outcomes of its responses, and lacks any ability to observe its environment beyond what is presented to it in text
0176: (
0177: Bender & Koller, 2020
0178: )
0179: .
0180: This can lead LLMs to not only make mistakes that seem unreasonable or humorous to people, but also to interpret instructions in ways that are nonsensical or unsafe for a particular embodiment or situation.
0181: Figure
0182: 1
0183: shows an example – a kitchen robot capable of executing skills such as “pick up the sponge”, ”pick up the coke can”, or ”go to the table” may be asked for help cleaning up a spill (“I spilled my drink, can you help?”).
0184: A language model responds with a reasonable narrative, but one that is not feasible or useful for the robot (“You could try using a vacuum cleaner” may be impossible if there is no vacuum in the scene or if the robot is incapable of operating a vacuum).
0185: The LLM has no knowledge of the current environment it is operating in, nor the capabilities of the agent that is following the instructions.
0186: With prompt engineering, a language model may be capable of splitting the high-level instruction into sub-tasks, but it cannot effectively do so without the context of what the robot is capable of given its abilities
0187: and
0188: the current state of the robot
0189: and
0190: the environment.
0191: In short, an LLM without grounding is divorced from reality.
0192: Unlike language models, robotic systems deal directly with perception and interaction, which leads to the question of whether such systems can effectively ground the semantic knowledge in language models.
0193: Motivated by this question, we study the problem of how to extract the knowledge in LLMs for enabling an embodied agent, such as a robot, to follow high-level textual instructions.
0194: The robot is equipped with a repertoire of learned skills for basic “atomic” behaviors, and these skills are capable of low-level perception and control.
0195: Our main insight is that, if we can simply make the LLM aware of the available and feasible repertoire of skills, this can provide it with an awareness of
0196: both
0197: the agent’s capabilities and the current state of the environment.
0198: In this sense, the skills will act as the LLMs “hands and eyes.”
0199: The main principle that we use to connect LLMs to physical tasks is to observe that, in addition to asking the LLM to simply interpret an instruction, we can use it to score the likelihood that an individual skill makes progress towards completing the high-level instruction.
0200: Furthermore, if each skill has an accompanying affordance function that quantifies how likely it is to succeed from the current state (such as a learned value function), its value can be used to weight the skill’s likelihood.
0201: This results in an elegant probabilistic interpretation of our method, where the LLM describes the probability that each skill aids in completing the instruction, and the affordance function describes the probability that each skill will succeed – combining the two provides the probability that each skill will perform the instruction
0202: successfully
0203: .
0204: The affordance functions make the LLM aware of the current scene, and constraining the completions to the skill descriptions makes the LLM aware of the robot’s capabilities.
0205: Furthermore, this combination results in a fully explainable sequence of steps that the robot will execute to accomplish an instruction – an interpretable plan that is expressed through natural language.
0206: In this work we present SayCan,
0207: an algorithm that extracts and leverages the knowledge within LLMs in physically-grounded tasks.
0208: The large language model (Say) provides a task-grounding to determine useful actions to accomplish a high-level goal and the robot-learned affordance functions (Can) provide a world-grounding to determine what is possible and to execute upon the plan.
0209: In particular, we use reinforcement learning (RL) as a way to learn language-conditioned value functions that provide affordances of what is possible in the world.
0210: We evaluate the proposed approach on about 100 real-world robotic tasks
0211: that involve a mobile robot accomplishing a large set of language instructions in a real kitchen in a zero-shot fashion.
0212: Our experiments validate that SayCan can execute temporally-extended, complex, and abstract instructions. Grounding the LLM in the real-world via affordances nearly doubles the performance over the non-grounded baselines.

0500: SayCan: Do As I Can, Not As I Say
0501: In this section we present our algorithm, SayCan, which adapts the knowledge contained in large language models (Say) to embodied tasks by leveraging real-world-grounded value functions (Can).
0502: The core idea behind our method is to use LLMs to provide task-grounding to an agent that is capable of executing short-horizon commands and, at the same time, use the agent’s value functions to provide the real-world grounding necessary for making the output of a LLM useful to the agent.
0503: At a high level, we start by querying the LLM about how it would split an instruction commanded by a user into smaller tasks that the agent is capable of executing. The initial user instruction can be ambiguous, abstract, or temporally extended, and it is the role of the LLM to disambiguate it to the level that it can be turned into a sequence of atomic steps that the robot can execute.
0504: We consider a set of available skills that the agent is able to perform
0505: and use their value functions that are able to specify which proposal skills are feasible given the current state of the agent and its environment. This effective filtering mechanism allows us to ground the knowledge extracted from the LLM to the real world of the agent’s embodiment.

0724: 3.2
0725: Connecting Large Language Models to Robots
0726: While large language models can draw on a wealth of knowledge learned from copious amounts of text, they will not necessarily break down high-level commands into low-level instructions that are suitable for robotic execution.
0727: If a language model were asked “how would a robot bring me an apple”, it may respond “a robot could go to a nearby store and purchase an apple for you”.
0728: Though this response is a reasonable completion for the prompt, it is not necessarily actionable to an embodied agent, which may have a fixed set of abilities and a narrow operational regime.
0729: Therefore, to adapt language models to our problem statement, we must somehow inform them that we specifically want the high-level instruction to be broken down into sequences of available low-level skills, and these skills should be appropriate based on a current observation of the world.
0730: One approach to alleviate such issues is careful prompt engineering
0731: (
0732: Brown et al., 2020
0733: ;
0734: Ouyang et al., 2022
0735: )
0736: , a technique to coax a language model to a specific response structure.
0737: Prompt engineering provides examples in the context text (“prompt”) for the model that specify the task and the response structure; the prompt used in this work is shown in Appendix
0738: C.2
0739: along with experiments ablating it.
0740: This makes solutions to the task more likely than superfluous completions.
0741: However, this is not enough to fully constrain the output to admissible primitive skills for an embodied agent, and indeed at times it can produce inadmissible actions or language that is not formatted in a way that is easy to parse into individual steps.
0742: Scoring language models open an avenue to constrained responses by outputting the probabilities assigned by a language model to fixed outputs.

1126: Implementing SayCan in a Robotic System
1127: In this section, we describe our implementation of SayCan in a robotic system that can perform a variety of user instructions in a kitchen environment. We first discuss the language conditioning for the control policies, then present details of how the policies and their corresponding value functions are trained, and finally describe the specifics of the real-world robotic system and the skills in our experimental evaluation.
1128: 4.1
1129: Language-Conditioned Robotic Control Policies
1130: To instantiate SayCan, we must provide it with a set of skills, each of which has a policy, a value function, and a short language description (e.g., “pick up the can”). These skills, value functions, and descriptions can be obtained in a variety of different ways.
1131: In our implementation, we train the individual skills either with image-based behavioral cloning, following the BC-Z method
1132: (
1133: Jang et al., 2021
1134: )
1135: , or reinforcement learning, following MT-Opt
1136: (
1137: Kalashnikov et al., 2021
1138: )
1139: .
1140: Regardless of how the skill’s policy is obtained, we utilize value functions trained via TD backups as the affordance model for that skill.
1141: While we find that the BC policies achieve higher success rates at the current stage of our data collection process, the value functions provided by the RL policies are crucial as an abstraction to translate control capabilities to a semantic understanding of the scene.
1142: In order to amortize the cost of training many skills, we utilize multi-task BC and multi-task RL, respectively, where instead of training a separate policy and value function per skill, we train multi-task policies and models that are
1143: conditioned
1144: on the language description.
1145: Note, however, that this description only corresponds to
1146: low level
1147: skills – it is still the role of the LLM in SayCan to interpret the high-level instruction and break it up into individual low level skill descriptions.
1148: To condition the policies on language, we utilize a pre-trained large sentence encoder language model
1149: (
1150: Cer et al., 2018
1151: )
1152: . We freeze the language model parameters during training and use the embeddings generated by passing in text descriptions of each skill. These text embeddings are used as the input to the policy and value function that specify which skill should be performed (see the details of the architectures used in the Appendix
1153: B.1
1154: ). Since the language model used to generate the text embeddings is not necessarily the same as the language model used for planning, SayCan is able to utilize different language models well suited for different abstraction levels – understanding planning with respect to many skills as opposed to expressing specific skills more granularly.
1155: 4.2
1156: Training the Low-Level Skills
1157: We utilize both BC and RL policy training procedures to obtain the language-conditioned policies and value functions, respectively. In the following, we describe the corresponding training procedures for each of the methods.
1158: Skill specification, reward functions, and action space.
1159: To complete the description of the underlying MDP that we consider, we provide the reward function as well as the skill specification that is used by the policies and value functions. As mentioned previously, for skill specification we use a set of short, natural language descriptions that are represented as language model embeddings. We utilize sparse reward functions with reward values of
1160: 1.0
1161: 1.0
1162: at the end of an episode if the language command was executed successfully, and
1163: 0.0
1164: 0.0
1165: otherwise.
1166: The success of language command execution is rated by humans where the raters are given a video of the robot performing the skill, together with the given instruction. If two out of the three raters agree that the skill was accomplished successfully, the episode is labelled with a positive reward.
1167: To additionally process the data, we also ask the raters to mark the episodes as unsafe (i.e., if the robot collided with the environment), undesirable (i.e., if the robot perturbed objects that were not relevant to the skill) or infeasible (i.e., if the skill cannot be done or is already accomplished). If any of these conditions are met, the episode is excluded from training.

1357: 5.3
1358: Metrics
1359: To understand the performance of the proposed method we measure two main metrics. The first is
1360: plan success rate
1361: , which measures whether the skills selected by the model are correct for the instruction, regardless of whether or not they actually successfully executed. We ask 3 human raters to indicate whether the plan generated by the model can achieve the instruction, and if 2 out of 3 raters agree that the plan is valid, it is marked a success. Note that for many instructions there may be multiple valid solutions. For example if the instruction is to “bring a sponge and throw away the soda can”, the plan can choose to bring sponge first or throw away the soda can first.
1362: The second metric is
1363: execution success rate
1364: , which measures whether the full SayCan system actually performs the desired instruction successfully. We ask 3 human raters to watch the robot execution.
1365: The raters are asked to answer the question “whether the robot achieves the task specified by the task string?”
1366: We mark an execution successful if 2 out of 3 raters agree that the execution is successful.

1540: Ablations: Removing Language and Affordance Groundings
1541: Ablating Language.
1542: To study the importance of the language model for interpreting complex instructions, we conduct an ablation experiment where we directly use the language-conditioned primitives (see Sections
1543: 4.1
1544: -
1545: 4.2
1546: ) and feed the full instruction
1547: i
1548: i
1549: directly into the text-conditioned primitive policy as though it were a skill label. Note that this approach is representative of standard RL or BC-based instruction following methods
1550: (
1551: Jang et al., 2021
1552: ;
1553: Stepputtis et al., 2020
1554: ;
1555: Nair et al., 2021
1556: ;
1557: Lynch & Sermanet, 2020
1558: )
1559: , which train a policy conditioned on a natural language command.
1560: The results in Table
1561: 4
1562: illustrate the necessity of the language grounding,
1563: in which we look at our learned BC-based policies alone.
1564: These policies were trained with a fixed set of language commands, and indeed we find anything outside of these commands fails completely, resulting in a 0% success rate even in the NL Single Primitive family.
1565: To alleviate this issue, we also try projecting the high-level instruction into the set of known language commands via the Universal Sentence Encoder (USE) embeddings
1566: (
1567: Cer et al., 2018
1568: )
1569: .
1570: To do this, we embed the instruction and all the tasks, along with the combinatorial set of sequences tasks (i.e., we consider “pick coke can” as well as “1. find coke can, 2. pick coke can” and so on), and select the highest cosine similarity trained instruction.
1571: This achieves 60% on the NL Single Primitives family, but 0% across all the other families.
1572: Ablating Value Functions.
1573: The results in Table
1574: 4
1575: illustrate the necessity of the affordance grounding
1576: through value functions.
1577: We compare SayCan to (1)
1578: No VF
1579: , which removes the value function grounding (i.e., choosing the maximum language score skill) and to (2)
1580: Generative
1581: , which uses the generative output of the LLM and then projects each planned skill to its maximal cosine similarity trained skill via pretrained Universal Sentence Encoder embeddings
1582: (
1583: Cer et al., 2018
1584: )
1585: .
1586: The latter in effect compares to
1587: the algorithm proposed in
1588: Huang et al. (2022)
1589: , which we note loses the explicit probabilities across options, and thus is less interpretable and cannot be combined with affordance probabilities.
1590: For
1591: Generative
1592: we also tried BERT embeddings
1593: (
1594: Devlin et al., 2018
1595: )
1596: , but found the performance of USE embeddings to be superior. The
1597: No VF
1598: and
1599: Generative
1600: approaches performed similarly in aggregate, achieving 38% and 43% planning success rate respectively, and worse than SayCan’s 70%.
1601: Both baselines performed similarly for the Long-Horizon, Embodiment, Crowd Sourced, and NL Single Primitive instruction families.
1602: These methods perform particularly poorly in the Embodiment experiments and in the Long-Horizon tasks, where the affordances are important in eliminating impossible skills that will lead to failure if triggered.
1603: Interestingly, the two ablations were not uniformly worse across all instruction families.
1604: In the instruction families NL Nouns, NL Verbs, and Structured Language, we find that the sequencing is more consistent with
1605: Generative
1606: , and that the limited number of verbs compared to objects means the loss in performance due to the projection is smaller.
1607: The nouns however are worse with the
1608: Generative
1609: approach, because the projection loses information about the overall query.
1610: For example, in the query “I just worked out, can you bring me a drink and a snack to recover?”, the generative model produces the sequence {find a drink, pick up the drink, go to the table, put down the drink, find a snack, pick up the snack, go to the table, put down the snack, done}. This sequence is reasonable, but when projecting “find a drink” it loses the earlier context that this should be a healthy drink and instead chooses “find a coke can”.
1611: This loss of context is not an issue for the scoring model however, which evaluates the probability of the skill given the full sequence so far, including the full original instruction.

1910: Conclusions, Limitations and Future Work
1911: We presented SayCan, a method that enables leveraging and grounding the rich knowledge in large language models to complete embodied tasks.
1912: For real-world grounding, we leverage pre-trained skills, which are then used to condition the model to choose natural language actions that are both feasible and contextually appropriate.
1913: More specifically, we use reinforcement learning as a way to learn value functions for the individual skills that provide affordances of what is possible in the world, and then use textual labels for these skills as potential responses that are scored by a language model.
1914: This combination results in a symbiotic relationship where the skills and their value functions can act as the language model’s “hands and eyes,” while the language model supplies high-level semantic knowledge about how to complete a task.
1915: We evaluated the proposed approach on a number of real-world robotic tasks that involve a mobile manipulator robot accomplishing a large set of long-horizon natural language instructions in a real kitchen.
1916: While SayCan presents a viable way to ground language models in agents’ affordances, it has a number of limitations.
1917: First, we expect this method to inherit the limitations and biases of LLMs
1918: (
1919: Bender et al., 2021
1920: ;
1921: Bommasani et al., 2021
1922: )
1923: ,
1924: including the dependence on the training data.
1925: Secondly, we observe that even though SayCan allows the users to interact with the agents using natural language commands, the primary bottleneck of the system is in the range and capabilities of the underlying skills.
1926: To illustrate this, we present representative failure cases in Appendix
1927: D
1928: .
1929: Future work that extends the repertoire of skills and improves their robustness would mitigate this limitation.
1930: In addition, at the current stage, the system is not easily able to react to situations where individual skills fail despite reporting a high value, though this could potentially be addressed by appropriate prompting of the language model for a correction.
1931: There are many other potential avenues for future work.
1932: A natural question that this work raises is how the information gained through grounding the LLM via real-world robotic experience can be leveraged to improve the language model itself, both in terms of its factuality and its ability to perform common-sense reasoning about real-world environments and physics. Furthermore, since our method uses generic value functions to score affordances, it is intriguing to consider what other sources of grounding could be incorporated in the same manner, such as non-robotic contexts.
1933: In the future, it is also interesting to examine whether
1934: natural language is the right ontology to use to program robots
1935: : natural language naturally incorporates contextual and semantic cues from the environment, and provides a level of abstraction which enables robots do decide on how to execute a strategy based on its own perception and affordances. At the same time, as opposed to, e.g., hindsight goal images
1936: (
1937: Chebotar et al., 2021
1938: )
1939: ,
1940: it requires supervision and it might not be the most descriptive medium for certain tasks.
1941: Lastly, SayCan presents a particular way of connecting and factorizing the challenges of language understanding and robotics, and many further extensions can be proposed. Ideas such as combining robot planning and language
1942: (
1943: Tellex et al., 2014
1944: )
1945: , using language models as a pre-training mechanism for policies
1946: (
1947: Reid et al., 2022
1948: )
1949: and many other ways of combining language and interaction
1950: (
1951: Nair et al., 2021
1952: ;
1953: Lynch & Sermanet, 2020
1954: ;
1955: Hill et al., 2020
1956: ;
1957: Wang et al., 2016
1958: )
1959: are exciting avenues for future research.

2686: RL and BC Policy Training
2687: Both RL and BC models were trained using 16 TPUv3 chips. RL models were trained for about 100 hours, and BC models were trained for about 27 hours. Both methods applied standard image augmentations (random brightness and contrast) as well as random cropping. The 640 x 512 input image was padded by 100 pixels left-right and 40 pixels top-down, then cropped back down to a 640 x 512 image, so as to allow for random spatial shifts without limiting the field of view.
2688: The RL model is trained via MT-Opt
2689: (
2690: Kalashnikov et al., 2021
2691: )
2692: , using a pool of 3000 CPU workers to collect episodes in the Everyday Robots simulator. Another 3000 CPU workers are used to compute target Q-values. Computing target Q-values outside the TPU allows the TPU to be used solely for computing gradient updates. Episode rewards are sparse and always 0 or 1, so the Q-function is updated using a log loss. 2724: B.3
2725: RL and BC Policy Evaluations
2726: In order to obtain the best possible manipulation capabilities for use in SayCan, we use a separate evaluation protocol for iterating on the RL and BC policies in the Mock Office Kitchen stations.
2727: Evaluations are divided by skill (pick up, knock over, place upright, open/close drawers, move object close to another one), and within each skill, 18-48 skills are sampled from a predetermined set of three objects. Object positions are randomized on each episode, with one or two objects serving as a distractor.
2728: The episode ends when 50 actions have been taken or the policy samples a terminate action. A human operator supervises multiple robots performing evaluation and performs scene resets as needed, and records each episode as a success or failure. Models whose per-skill performance outperforms prior models are ”graduated” to the same evaluation protocol in the real kitchen, and then integrated into SayCan. We found that despite the domain shift from Mock Office Kitchen stations to the actual kitchen counter and drawers, higher success rates on mock stations usually corresponded to higher success rates in the real kitchen setting.

2731: shows the development of the manipulation skills over time. It reports the per-skill success rate, the average success rate across all skills, and the number of instructions the policy was trained on.
2732: Over the course of the project, we increased the number of skills evaluated, from 1 instruction in April 2021 to hundreds of instructions at time of publication over the course of 366 real-world model evaluations.

2978: C.2
2979: LLM
2980: The LLM uses prompt engineering and a strict response structure to score skills.
2981: But, as SayCan as a whole requires affordances from a world embodiment, it is not straightforward to optimize this structure and tune parameters quickly.
2982: Thus we built a language-based simulator which, given a query and a solution sequence of skills, outputs affordances consistent with the query and solution. It also generates consistent distractor affordances to ensure robustness.
2983: The simulator then verifies that SayCan recovers the correct solution and tests how confident SayCan is in the correct solutions.
2984: In Table
2985: 5
2986: we test the effect of the number of examples in the prompt on the planning success rate in the language-based simulator (over 50 demonstrative instructions).
2987: We show a success rate with and without requiring the plan to terminate; without examples we found the LLM was unlikely to issue a “done” phase.
2988: With no examples SayCan is able to successfully plan 54% without the done condition, but only 10% with the done condition.
2989: Though it makes mistakes, clearly some information is already imbued within the language model.
2990: It is able to correctly solve “Can I have a redbull please?” and “Move the chips bag from the table to the counter.”.
2991: With only one example the LLM quickly improves in both planning rates, though still fails to terminate the plan occasionally.
2992: After only four examples the LLM is performant, planning 82% of the queries correctly, though the remaining errors are largely within a single instruction family: Long-Horizon.
2993: Finally, the prompt used in this work, Listing
2994: 1
2995: , involved 17 examples and recovered 88% of the solutions correctly.
2996: We note here briefly a few lessons learned in prompt engineering and structuring the final prompt.
2997: Providing explicit numbers between steps (e.g., 1., 2., instead of combining skills with “and then” or other phrases) improved performance, as did breaking each step into a separate line (e.g. adding a “\n” between steps).
2998: Examples which overly include objects used in the actual planning tend to bias results to those objects (e.g., if every example is about apples then the apple scoring will be off in planning).
2999: Phrasing of the natural language names of skills and objects is important due to the auto-regressive nature of the LLM scoring – skills and objects should be naturally named and errors such as misspellings or mismatches in “a” vs “an” can be problematic.
3000: Notably, since user generated instructions are taken as given such fragility is not issues for the input, allowing a robustness to user queries.
