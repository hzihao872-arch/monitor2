0023: Abstract
0024: Vision-language-action (VLA) models have improved robotic manipulation, yet
0025: their policies remain vulnerable to compounding errors, unseen scene changes,
0026: and off-trajectory states. Reinforcement learning (RL) offers a promising
0027: route to refine pretrained VLA policies, but its effectiveness depends on
0028: informative rewards. Sparse success signals make exploration inefficient,
0029: whereas manually engineered dense rewards are costly, brittle, and
0030: task-specific. Learned visual reward models provide dense feedback, but
0031: existing robotic reward models often rely on static before–after observations
0032: and struggle to capture execution context and failure semantics. This leads to
0033: temporal ambiguity
0034: , where similar observations correspond to different
0035: latent progress due to hidden contact events, repeated phases, or reordered
0036: subgoals, and limits their ability to distinguish robustness-preserving
0037: variations from task-invalid failures under out-of-distribution (OOD)
0038: execution. We introduce
0039: Robo-Dopamine 2.0
0040: , a history- and OOD-aware process
0041: reward model that retains a pairwise prediction interface.
0042: Robo-Dopamine 2.0 improves progress estimation under these conditions through two
0043: complementary designs: (1) a history-conditioned pairwise reward formulation
0044: that uses source-aligned reference panels for synthetic OOD queries and
0045: observed rollout history for online queries, while preserving the actual queried
0046: endpoints;
0047: and (2) an OOD-aware signed progress space that organizes positive,
0048: robustness-preserving, and negative states into a unified progress
0049: representation. To improve optimization of fine-grained progress ordering, we
0050: further develop a
0051: Signed-Hop Curriculum
0052: with transition-aware replay
0053: that progressively learns coarse execution ordering before fine-grained
0054: progress calibration. We also construct an OOD trajectory dataset and a
0055: five-family benchmark for comprehensive evaluation. Reference panels improve mean visual order consistency (VOC) from 0.967 to
0056: 0.986 and OOD-robust VOC from 0.906 to 0.958. Under the same 400K pairwise-reward budget, Signed-Hop training with 25%
0057: replay reaches 0.9872 mean VOC, compared with 0.9858 for a matched-pool
0058: shuffled control. In downstream RL, the full model reaches 86.8% mean
0059: RoboTwin success and 71/80 successful real-world insertions.

0072: Introduction
0073: Large-scale imitation learning and vision-language-action models have
0074: substantially improved visuomotor policy generalization from diverse
0075: demonstrations
0076: (
0077: Brohan et al. 2023
0078: ;
0079: Zitkovich et al. 2023
0080: ;
0081: Kim et al. 2025b
0082: )
0083: .
0084: However, long-horizon, contact-rich manipulation remains challenging due to
0085: compounding errors, unseen scene changes, and off-trajectory states that
0086: require recovery.
0087: Reinforcement learning can improve policies through interaction, but its
0088: practical deployment on real robots critically depends on rewards that are
0089: dense, task-relevant, and reliable beyond the demonstration distribution.
0090: Sparse terminal rewards make exploration inefficient, whereas handcrafted
0091: dense rewards are brittle, task-specific, and expensive to
0092: engineer
0093: (
0094: Lee et al. 2021
0095: ;
0096: Wang et al. 2024
0097: ;
0098: Sontakke et al. 2023
0099: )
0100: .
0101: Recent visual and language-conditioned reward models address this bottleneck by
0102: learning dense feedback from observations and task descriptions
0103: (
0104: Ma et al. 2023b
0105: ;
0106: Ma et al. 2023a
0107: ;
0108: Baumli et al. 2023
0109: ;
0110: Wang et al. 2024
0111: )
0112: ; robot-specific methods
0113: further introduce stage- or process-aware progress estimation
0114: (
0115: Hung et al. 2025
0116: ;
0117: Tan et al. 2026
0118: ;
0119: Chen et al. 2026a
0120: ;
0121: Zhang et al. 2026
0122: )
0123: .
0124: Nevertheless, existing approaches remain limited in accurately evaluating
0125: execution progress under challenging manipulation scenarios.
0126: First
0127: , static endpoint pairs suffer from
0128: temporal aliasing
0129: : visually
0130: similar states may correspond to different latent progress due to hidden
0131: contact events, repeated phases, or reordered subgoals
0132: (
0133: Ni et al. 2022
0134: ;
0135: Guhur et al. 2023
0136: ;
0137: Fu et al. 2024
0138: )
0139: .
0140: Second
0141: , robust execution requires distinguishing semantics-preserving
0142: variations from true failures: occlusions or distractors may preserve task
0143: progress, whereas failed grasps, wrong-object interactions, and incomplete
0144: releases should receive negative feedback. However, successful
0145: in-distribution trajectories provide limited supervision for learning such
0146: failure-aware distinctions.
0147: To address these challenges, we introduce
0148: Robo-Dopamine 2.0
0149: , a
0150: history- and OOD-aware pairwise reward learning framework that retains the
0151: pairwise progress formulation used in prior process reward
0152: modeling
0153: (
0154: Tan et al. 2026
0155: )
0156: . Robo-Dopamine 2.0 improves phase-aware and
0157: failure-aware progress estimation through two complementary designs:
0158: (1) a history-conditioned pairwise reward formulation
0159: that
0160: uses same-episode expert history for standard queries and observed rollout
0161: history for online queries, while synthetic OOD queries use phase-aligned
0162: successful-reference panels without replacing their edited endpoints; and
0163: (2) an OOD-aware signed progress space
0164: that
0165: organizes execution states into positive, robustness-preserving, and negative
0166: branches, enabling the model to represent valid progress,
0167: semantics-preserving variations, failures, and recovery within a unified
0168: pairwise training interface.
0169: Furthermore, we introduce a
0170: Signed-Hop Curriculum
0171: with
0172: transition-aware replay to progressively learn execution ordering.
0173: The curriculum first captures coarse-grained ordering between successful,
0174: robust, and failed states, and then refines fine-grained progress differences.
0175: The replay strategy preserves informative large-transition pairs during
0176: training, improving progress calibration under diverse execution scenarios.
0177: We evaluate Robo-Dopamine 2.0 through a trajectory-level visual order consistency (VOC)
0178: evaluation across five benchmark families covering in-distribution, temporal,
0179: and OOD scenarios.
0180: Our contributions are summarized as follows:
0181: •
0182: We propose
0183: Robo-Dopamine 2.0
0184: , a history- and OOD-aware pairwise reward framework that supports source-reference context for synthetic OOD queries and observed rollout context for online reward inference, together with a signed progress space for valid progress, robustness, failure, and recovery.
0185: •
0186: We develop a
0187: Signed-Hop Curriculum
0188: with transition-aware replay that
0189: improves learning of global execution ordering and fine-grained progress
0190: calibration.
0191: •
0192: We present a trajectory-level visual order consistency (VOC)
0193: evaluation across five benchmark families covering in-distribution, temporal,
0194: and OOD scenarios, including controlled studies of static versus
0195: history-conditioned reward modeling.

2027: Signed-Hop Curriculum
2028: Signed-Hop first learns global geometry from large-Hop pairs, then refines local
2029: calibration with small and zero-Hop pairs while replaying large-Hop examples.
2030: Under the fixed 400K budget, 25% replay reaches 0.9872 average VOC, exceeding
2031: the matched-pool shuffled control (0.9858) and no replay (0.9866). The shuffled
2032: control uses exactly the same sample pool as the replay curriculum, isolating
2033: ordering from data composition; the no-replay variant isolates retention of
2034: large-Hop geometry during local refinement.

2236: Conclusion
2237: Robo-Dopamine 2.0 combines history-conditioned pairwise prediction, an OOD-aware signed
2238: progress space, and a Signed-Hop curriculum in one reward interface. Standard
2239: queries use same-episode expert context, synthetic OOD queries retain edited
2240: endpoints with aligned successful-reference panels, and online queries use
2241: observed rollout history. The signed space models valid progress,
2242: robustness-preserving variations, task-invalid failures, and recovery, while
2243: the curriculum learns large transitions before fine-grained calibration.
2244: Offline and downstream RL results show complementary gains from temporal
2245: context, OOD supervision, curriculum ordering, and replay across offline
2246: ordering, simulation, and real-world manipulation settings. The same pairwise
2247: interface supports offline progress estimation and closed-loop reward shaping.
2248: Future work will broaden task, OOD, and real-world evaluation.

3493: Negative Counterfactual Operators
3494: Negative operators change a task-relevant execution condition while keeping the
3495: source robot pose and trajectory phase fixed.
3496: Wrong object.
3497: If the gripper is empty or approaching the target, the task target in the grasp
3498: corridor is replaced by a visually distinct non-target object, while the
3499: original target is relocated to a plausible position outside the corridor.
3500: This makes the impending interaction correspond to the wrong object rather
3501: than an empty grasp. If the gripper is occupied, only the held content is
3502: replaced by an object with a different category, dominant color, and
3503: silhouette. The original target remains naturally situated elsewhere whenever
3504: visible.
3505: Empty grasp.
3506: The defining condition is an empty region between the gripper fingers. If the
3507: gripper is approaching an object, the target and any object intersecting the
3508: opening, closing region, or forward grasp corridor are moved laterally outside
3509: the grasp path. If the gripper already contains an object, that object is
3510: removed without changing the gripper pose or opening. Only the minimal local
3511: region is reconstructed to recover the visible fingers, interior background,
3512: shadows, and reflections. The target is returned to its source-scene location
3513: when this location is supported by the trajectory references.
3514: Non-release.
3515: The
3516: non-release
3517: operator converts a successful release or near-release
3518: state into a continued-holding state. If the target has already left the
3519: gripper, the same target is inserted back into the current gripper and its
3520: released instance is removed to avoid duplication. The gripper pose is not
3521: replaced by an earlier source pose. If the target is already held, its identity
3522: and appearance are preserved while the continued-holding configuration remains
3523: unchanged.
3524: Target replacement.
3525: Only the instruction-referenced target is replaced. The replacement preserves
3526: the original target’s approximate image footprint, location, local support or
3527: grasp relation, and occlusion order, but differs clearly in category and
3528: silhouette. All surrounding objects, articulated states, and robot geometry
3529: remain fixed. The resulting state therefore preserves the local spatial
3530: arrangement while violating task-object identity. Unlike
3531: wrong object
3532: , this operator changes the identity of the
3533: instruction-referenced scene target rather than the identity of the object
3534: currently approached or held by the gripper.
3535: Robustness-Preserving Operators
3536: Robustness operators change image appearance without changing task semantics and
3537: therefore retain the same progress value as their aligned source states.
3538: Foreground occlusion.
3539: A plausible close-range occluder, such as a hand, board, box, or cloth region,
3540: is inserted in front of the critical interaction area. The occluder may
3541: partially hide the target or gripper, but it may not cover the complete image.
3542: The underlying robot–object state, target identity, contact state, task
3543: outcome, and all pre-existing scene objects remain unchanged. The occluder is
3544: placed according to the queried camera’s depth ordering rather than used to
3545: redraw the hidden interaction into a different state.
3546: Background change.
3547: One or two small modifications are applied to secondary background regions,
3548: such as a minor layout adjustment or local appearance variation away from the
3549: interaction area. Target identity and location, robot and gripper state,
3550: articulated-object state, and the geometry of the critical contact region remain
3551: unchanged.
3552: Distractor addition or removal.
3553: A small number of non-target objects are added to or removed from background or
3554: secondary regions. Added objects must have plausible scale, support, material,
3555: illumination, and shadow; removed regions are completed using neighboring scene
3556: texture. New objects are not placed adjacent to the gripper or target, and no
3557: task-relevant object or receptacle may be removed.
3558: Non-target color change.
3559: A small, natural change in color, saturation, or brightness is applied to one or
3560: two non-target objects. No object is added, removed, moved, rotated, or
3561: replaced. The target color and identity, object geometry, scene layout, robot
3562: state, contact relations, and task outcome remain fixed. When color is part of
3563: the instruction, the target color is protected by both operator selection and
3564: the prompt.
3565: Camera-Specific Geometric Constraints
3566: The same semantic edit requires different geometric instructions under the head
3567: and wrist cameras. We therefore append a camera-specific block to each prompt.
3568: Head camera.
3569: The head view exposes the global scene layout and the projected grasp corridor.
3570: For
3571: empty grasp

3573: region between the fingers, directly below the gripper, and along its projected
3574: approach path. For
3575: wrong object

3578: non-release

3581: Wrist cameras.
3582: Wrist views are treated as local close-up observations. Editing is restricted
3583: to the gripper, target, and immediately adjacent image region. The prompt
3584: prohibits expanding an object surface that is occluded in the queried wrist
3585: view, copying geometry from the head camera, or producing the opposite wrist’s
