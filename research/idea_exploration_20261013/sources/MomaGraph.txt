0041: Abstract
0042: Mobile manipulators in households must both navigate and manipulate. This requires a compact, semantically rich scene representation that captures
0043: where
0044: objects are,
0045: how
0046: they function, and
0047: which parts
0048: are actionable. Scene graphs are a natural choice, yet prior work often separates spatial and functional relations, treats scenes as static snapshots without object states or temporal updates, and overlooks information most relevant for accomplishing the current task. To address these limitations, we introduce
0049: MomaGraph
0050: , a unified scene representation for embodied agents that integrates spatial-functional relationships and part-level interactive elements. However, advancing such a representation requires both suitable data and rigorous evaluation, which have been largely missing. We thus contribute
0051: MomaGraph-Scenes
0052: , the first large-scale dataset of richly annotated, task-driven scene graphs in household environments, along with
0053: MomaGraph-Bench
0054: , a systematic evaluation suite spanning six reasoning capabilities from high-level planning to fine-grained scene understanding. Built upon this foundation, we further develop
0055: MomaGraph-R1
0056: , a 7B vision–language model trained with reinforcement learning on
0057: MomaGraph-Scenes
0058: .
0059: MomaGraph-R1
0060: predicts task-oriented scene graphs and serves as a zero-shot task planner under a
0061: Graph-then-Plan
0062: framework. Extensive experiments demonstrate that our model achieves state-of-the-art results among open-source models, reaching
0063: 71.6%
0064: accuracy on the benchmark (
0065: +11.4%
0066: over the best baseline), while generalizing across public benchmarks and transferring effectively to real-robot experiments.

0068: Overview of the
0069: MomaGraph
0070: . Given a task instruction,
0071: MomaGraph
0072: constructs a task-specific scene graph that highlights relevant objects and parts along with their spatial-functional relationships, enabling the robot to perform spatial understanding and task planning.
0073: 1
0074: Introduction
0075: When mobile manipulators
0076: (
0077: Qiu et al., 2024
0078: ;
0079: Honerkamp et al., 2024a
0080: ;
0081: Wu et al., 2023
0082: ;
0083: Zhang et al., 2024a
0084: )
0085: enter household environments, they face the fundamental challenge of understanding how the environment works, which objects are interactive, and how they can be used. In other words, such robots must not only be capable of navigating through the home, but also manipulate objects within it. While navigation requires modeling the overall spatial layout, manipulation demands capturing more fine-grained object affordances
0086: (
0087: Ju et al., 2024
0088: ;
0089: Zhu et al., 2025
0090: )
0091: . This naturally raises a central question:
0092: What is the most effective, compact, and semantically rich representation of an indoor scene?
0093: An intuitive answer is the
0094: scene graph
0095: , which organizes objects and their relationships in a scene through a graph structure
0096: (
0097: Armeni et al., 2019
0098: ;
0099: Koch et al., 2024a
0100: ;
0101: Koch et al., 2024b
0102: )
0103: and has shown great potential in various downstream robotic applications
0104: (
0105: Rana et al., 2023
0106: ;
0107: Werby et al., 2024
0108: ;
0109: Ekpo et al., 2024
0110: )
0111: .
0112: However, existing scene graphs suffer from notable limitations. (1) Their edges typically encode only a single type of relationship, either spatial
0113: (
0114: Jatavallabhula et al., 2023
0115: ;
0116: Gu et al., 2024a
0117: ;
0118: Loo et al., 2025
0119: )
0120: or functional
0121: (
0122: Zhang et al., 2025
0123: ;
0124: Dong et al., 2021
0125: )
0126: (
0127: e.g., a remote controlling a TV, a knob adjusting parameters
0128: ). Relying solely on spatial relationships captures geometric layout but overlooks operability, while relying solely on functional relationships ignores spatial constraints, leading to incomplete and less actionable structures. (2) Most existing methods
0129: (
0130: Wu et al., 2021
0131: ;
0132: Takmaz et al., 2025
0133: ;
0134: Zhang et al., 2021
0135: )
0136: are limited to static scenes and struggle to adapt to dynamic environments where object positions change or object states change. (3) They lack task relevance, as they fail to emphasize information directly tied to task execution, thereby reducing efficiency and effectiveness. In contrast, cognitive science research
0137: (
0138: Uithol et al., 2021
0139: ;
0140: Kondyli et al., 2020
0141: ;
0142: Castanheira et al., 2025
0143: )
0144: shows that human perception in new environments is both dynamic and task-oriented. Humans do not process all information equally; instead, they flexibly adjust their attention according to the current task. This process is similar to browsing a map on an iPad: people first take a broad view to roughly locate the area of interest, and then zoom in to focus on the specific details needed for the task.
0145: Motivated by these insights, we emphasize that
0146: an ideal scene graph should integrate both spatial and functional relationships, include fine-grained object parts as nodes, making the representation compact, adaptive to dynamic changes, and highly aligned with task instructions, thus providing a more concrete guidance for embodied perception and task planning.
0147: To achieve this goal, we present
0148: MomaGraph
0149: , a novel scene representation specifically designed for embodied agents. It is the first to unify spatial and functional relationships while introducing part-level interactive nodes, providing a more fine-grained, compact, and task-relevant structured representation than existing approaches. To support this representation, we build
0150: MomaGraph-Scenes
0151: , the first dataset that jointly models spatial and functional relationships with part-level annotations, encompassing multi-view observations, executed actions, and their interactive object parts, and task-aligned scene graph annotations.
0152: Building on this foundation, we propose
0153: MomaGraph-R1
0154: , a 7B vision–language model (VLM) trained with the DAPO
0155: (
0156: Yu et al., 2025
0157: )
0158: reinforcement learning algorithm on
0159: MomaGraph-Scenes
0160: . We design a graph-alignment reward function to guide the model toward constructing accurate, task-oriented scene graphs.
0161: MomaGraph-R1
0162: not only predicts scene graphs but also serves as a zero-shot task planner within a
0163: Graph-then-Plan
0164: framework: the model first generates a structured scene graph as an intermediate representation and then performs task planning based on this graph, significantly improving reasoning effectiveness and interpretability.
0165: Despite progress in task-graph planning
0166: (
0167: Agia et al., 2022
0168: )
0169: , the community still lacks a unified benchmark to systematically evaluate whether and how task-oriented scene graphs improve planning performance. To address this gap, we introduce
0170: MomaGraph-Bench
0171: , a comprehensive evaluation suite that systematically assesses six key reasoning capabilities, spanning from high-level task planning to fine-grained scene understanding.
0172: In summary, our work makes the following key contributions:
0173: •
0174: We propose
0175: MomaGraph
0176: , the first scene graph representation that jointly models spatial and functional relationships while incorporating part-level interactive nodes, providing a compact, dynamic, and task-aligned knowledge structure for embodied intelligence.
0177: •
0178: We construct
0179: MomaGraph-Scenes
0180: , the first large-scale dataset of richly annotated, task-driven scene graphs in household environments, and build
0181: MomaGraph-Bench
0182: , a unified evaluation suite that systematically measures the impact of scene graph representations on task planning across six core reasoning capabilities.
0183: •
0184: We develop
0185: MomaGraph-R1
0186: , a 7B vision-language model that leverages reinforcement learning to optimize spatial–functional reasoning, enabling zero-shot planning in a
0187: Graph-then-Plan
0188: paradigm.
0189: •
0190: MomaGraph-R1
0191: surpasses all open-source baseline models, delivering substantial gains across public benchmarks and translating these improvements into strong generalization and effectiveness in real-world robotic experiments.

Prior approaches such as SayPlan
0284: (
0285: Ahn et al., 2022
0286: )
0287: assume access to a reliable 3D scene graph, which is often unrealistic in practice. To overcome this gap, we propose the
0288: Graph-then-Plan
0289: strategy, which first generates task-specific scene graphs as an intermediate structured representation before high-level planning. 0290: (

0292: ;
0293: Ekpo et al., 2024
0294: )
0295: that either assume reliable scene graphs or treat graph construction and planning as separate modules, our approach enables a single VLM to jointly generate structured, task-oriented scene graphs and perform high-level planning.

0297: Preliminary Findings and Motivation Experiments
0298: To ground our analysis, before the full evaluations we perform two motivating experiments on the
0299: MomaGraph-Bench
0300: . These comparisons are designed to validate our motivation and design principles, and to reveal why our proposed model is essential for embodied task planning. In this section, we aim to answer the following questions.
0301: 3.1
0302: Are VLMs Reliable for Direct Planning Without Scene Graphs?
0303: To examine whether direct planning from visual inputs is reliable even for strong closed-source VLMs, we design controlled evaluations on real-world household tasks such as
0304: “Open the window”
0305: and
0306: “Obtain clean boiled water”
0307: . In these scenarios, models must reason over functional relationships, spatial constraints, and multi-step dependencies (e.g., plug-in before activation, filtration before boiling). As shown in Fig.
0308: 2
0309: , despite their scale, closed-source VLMs like GPT-5 produce incorrect or incomplete plans, missing prerequisite steps, or misidentifying interaction types. In contrast, our
0310: Graph-then-Plan
0311: approach, which first generates a task-specific scene graph and then performs planning, consistently produces correct and complete action sequences aligned with ground-truth logic. This demonstrates that incorporating structured scene representations significantly enhances planning accuracy and robustness beyond what direct planning can achieve.
0312: Figure 2:
0313: Direct planning often fails even for strong closed-source models like GPT-5, producing wrong actions or missing key steps, while our
0314: Graph-then-Plan
0315: approach with structured scene graphs enables accurate and complete task sequences aligned with ground truth.
0316: Preliminary Findings 1
0317: •
0318: In contrast to directly relying on vision-language models for task planning from raw scene images, our
0319: Graph-then-Plan
0320: strategy—which incorporates task-oriented scene graph generation as an intermediate structured representation prior to high-level planning, substantially improves both the accuracy and robustness of task planning.

0886: 5.1
0887: MomaGraph-Scenes Dataset
0888: Existing scene graph datasets for 3D indoor environments are often constrained to a single relationship: some focus exclusively on
0889: spatial layouts
0890: of objects
0891: (
0892: Armeni et al., 2019
0893: ;
0894: Koch et al., 2024b
0895: )
0896: , while others emphasize
0897: functional interactions
0898: (
0899: Dong et al., 2021
0900: ;
0901: Zhang et al., 2025
0902: )
0903: . However, these scene graph representations that are restricted to a single relationship type are insufficient for embodied agents, as task execution in household environments requires reasoning about both
0904: where objects are
0905: and
0906: how they can be used
0907: . To address these limitations, we introduce
0908: MomaGraph-Scenes
0909: , the first dataset designed to provide a more comprehensive and task-relevant scene representation.
0910: MomaGraph-Scenes
0911: jointly encodes
0912: spatial relationships
0913: and
0914: functional relationships
0915: , covering
0916: 9 spatial relationship types and 6 functional relationship types
0917: , explicitly representing interactive elements such as handles and buttons. Our dataset consists of approximately 1,050 task-oriented subgraphs and 6278 multi-view RGB images, collected from a combination of manually collected real-world data, re-annotated existing datasets
0918: (
0919: Zhang et al., 2025
0920: ;
0921: Delitzas et al., 2024
0922: )
0923: , and simulated environments built with AI2-THOR
0924: (
0925: Kolve et al., 2017
0926: )
0927: . These samples span more than
0928: 350 diverse household scenes
0929: and encompass
0930: 93 distinct task instructions
0931: . Compared with prior datasets, our annotations are significantly more detailed, and capturing interaction semantics at both the object and part levels. This broad coverage ensures rich variability in scene layouts, object configurations, and interaction types, supporting robust learning and evaluation of embodied reasoning.
0932: 5.1.1
0933: Dataset Annotation
0934: Multi-View Observation.
0935: The multi-view images provided for each graph are not constrained to always contain every relevant object within each single view. We also do not impose restrictions on the number of viewpoints or their exact configurations. This flexible setup better reflects realistic perception conditions, where embodied agents must reason across partial and diverse observations to build consistent scene graph representations.
0936: Task Instruction.
0937: It is worth noting that the task instructions in our dataset do not explicitly mention all the objects required to accomplish the task. Instead, they are expressed in simple and natural forms (e.g., “Fill the bathtub”), where the relevant objects such as the
0938: bathtub
0939: ,
0940: faucet
0941: , and
0942: button
0943: must be inferred by the model. This design encourages the model to learn how to ground natural instructions into the appropriate set of objects and relationships, rather than relying on object names being explicitly stated.

0977: Functional Relationships.
0978: We define a functional relationship as the ability of one object to change the state of another object.
0979: In indoor environments, common tasks can be broadly categorized as
0980: Parameter Adjustment, Device Control, Open/Close the Cabinet or Door, Water Flow Control, Power Supply, and Assembly
0981: .
0982: Accordingly, we identify six major types:
0983: [OPEN OR CLOSE], [ADJUST], [CONTROL], [ACTIVATE], [POWER BY], [PAIR WITH].
0984: Notably, [PAIR WITH] does not alter the internal state of objects but instead modifies their spatial configuration, which is essential for assembly tasks
0985: (
0986: Qi et al., 2025
0987: )
0988: .
0989: Since such tasks are critical for robotic interaction and task planning, we explicitly include [PAIR WITH] as a functional relationship.
0990: Through this definition, our dataset extends beyond physical and electronic interactions to encompass fine-grained reasoning about assembly and pairing, enhancing its utility for downstream action execution and planning.
0991: •
0992: Spatial Relationships.
0993: Capture geometric dependencies between objects and parts.
0994: The dataset primarily annotates:
0995: Directional:
0996: [LEFT OF], [RIGHT OF], [IN FRONT OF], [BEHIND], [HIGHER THAN], [LOWER THAN].
0997: Distance-based:
0998: [CLOSE], [FAR], [TOUCHING].

1008: 5.2
1009: MomaGraph Benchmark and Evaluation
1010: We introduce
1011: MomaGraph-Bench
1012: , the first benchmark that jointly evaluates fine-grained scene understanding and task planning abilities across diverse levels of difficulty. Our design principle for
1013: MomaGraph-Bench
1014: is to evaluate whether advances in scene understanding provide tangible improvements in downstream task planning and reasoning. Our evaluation framework examines six essential reasoning capabilities in four tiers of difficulty levels: (1)
1015: Action Sequence Reasoning
1016: , (2)
1017: Spatial Reasoning
1018: , (3)
1019: Object Affordance Reasoning
1020: , (4)
1021: Precondition & Effect Reasoning
1022: , (5)
1023: Goal Decomposition
1024: , and (6)
1025: Visual Correspondence
1026: (with concrete examples shown in Fig.
1027: 4
1028: ).
1029: •
1030: Action Sequence Reasoning
1031: : examines whether models understand the order and dependency of actions and can plan efficient sequences.
1032: •
1033: Spatial Reasoning
1034: : focuses on reasoning over spatial relations such as
1035: left_of
1036: or
1037: in_front_of
1038: , judging reachability, and selecting the most suitable object among candidates.
1039: •
1040: Object Affordance Reasoning
1041: : evaluates whether models can infer the functionality of objects (e.g., knobs can be turned, cabinets can be opened), match objects to task requirements, and reason about indirect tool use.
1042: •
1043: Precondition & Effect Reasoning
1044: : assesses whether models understand the preconditions and effects of actions, such as a door needing to be closed before it can be opened, and can predict possible side effects.
1045: •
1046: Goal Decomposition
1047: : measures the ability to break down complex tasks into sub-goals, prioritize them, and determine parallel versus sequential execution strategies.
1048: •
1049: Visual Correspondence (extended capability)
1050: : tests whether models can maintain object consistency across multiple views and integrate information under viewpoint changes.
1051: MomaGraph-Bench
1052: is formulated as a multi-choice VQA task which comprises 294 diverse indoor scenes with 1,446 multi-view images, featuring 352 task-oriented scene graphs spanning 1,315 instances that range from simple step object manipulation(Tier 1) to complex multi-step replanning (Tier 4) scenarios (detailed breakdown in Appendix
1053: A.4
1054: ).
1055: MomaGraph-Bench
1056: offers the most comprehensive assessment for embodied agents’ capacity to generalize across tasks and scenarios. To ensure that the evaluation truly reflects generalization rather than memorization, all scenarios are drawn from entirely unseen environments.

1322: interactions (e.g., turning on the TV, turning off a light).

1324: Prior to execution, the robot performs active perception by adjusting its head pose to acquire multi-view observations.
1325: MomaGraph-R1
1326: processes these observations together with the task instruction to generate a task-specific subgraph, which explicitly encodes the relevant objects and their spatial–functional relationships, see more deployment details in
1327: B.3

1331: MomaGraph-R1
1332: then functions as a task planner, producing a structured action sequence. 1333: MomaGraph-R1
1334: .

1344: To provide rigorous quantitative validation of our system’s robustness, we conduct a comprehensive evaluation on a complex multi-step long-horizon task. 1351: Task Setup.
1352: We evaluate the following natural language instruction that requires sequential reasoning and manipulation:
1353: “I need better lighting. Turn on the light closest to the remote so I can find it and turn on the monitor to watch.”
1354: To assess system robustness, we conducted 10 experimental trials, changing the camera viewpoint in each trial.
1355: This task requires spatial reasoning (finding the switch and the remote), functional understanding (linking switches, lights, remote, monitor), and state-dependent planning (lighting affects perception). 1357: As shown in Figure

1360: The main failure modes were: (1)spatial relation errors or missing nodes during graph generation; and (2) action sequencing errors in the planning phase, suggesting that the system sometimes plans the right actions but in a suboptimal order.
1361: These results demonstrate that
1362: MomaGraph
1363: remains robust across multiple reasoning and execution stages, achieving a 70% overall success rate on a complex multi-step task. This validates the system’s reliability under realistic long-horizon conditions where errors can compound across stages.
1364: 7
1365: Conclusion
1366: This work addresses to the fundamental limitations of existing scene graphs for embodied agents: reliance on a single type of relationship, inability to adapt to dynamic environments, and lack of task relevance. To overcome these issues, we introduce
1367: MomaGraph
1368: , a novel scene representation that unifies spatial and functional scene graphs with interactive elements. To learn this representation, we construct a large-scale dataset
1369: MomaGraph-Scenes
1370: and propose
1371: MomaGraph-R1
1372: , a 7B VLM trained with reinforcement learning, which predicts task-oriented scene graphs and serves as a zero-shot task planner under a
1373: Graph-then-Plan
1374: framework. Furthermore, we design the
1375: MomaGraph-Bench
1376: , a comprehensive benchmark that rigorously evaluates both fine-grained reasoning and high-level planning. Through extensive experiments, we demonstrate that our approach achieves state-of-the-art performance among open source models, remains competitive with closed source systems, and transfers effectively to public benchmarks and real robot experiments. We hope that
1377: MomaGraph
1378: will serve as a foundation for advancing scene representations, fostering stronger connections between the spatial VLM and robotics communities, and ultimately enabling more intelligent and adaptive embodied agents.

2197: A.4.1
2198: Benchmark Design
2199: To rigorously evaluate spatial–functional reasoning and task planning capabilities, we design a comprehensive multi-choice VQA benchmark based on the scenes and tasks in our dataset. Rather than manually crafting all questions, we leverage large vision–language models (VLMs) to generate them in a scalable and diverse manner. Specifically, we provide the model with structured prompts describing the scene images, state-aware scene graph, and task instructions, and instruct it to produce question–answer pairs that probe different reasoning skills, such as spatial relation understanding, affordance inference, precondition reasoning, and goal decomposition. To ensure the reliability and correctness of the benchmark, all generated questions and answers undergo several rounds of manual verification, during which ambiguous or low-quality samples are refined or removed.
2200: Moreover, since the benchmark is formulated as a multi-choice VQA task with clearly defined correct answers, it does not require complex evaluation metrics. Model performance can be directly measured by simple accuracy — i.e., the proportion of correctly answered questions — which provides an intuitive and reliable indicator of spatial–functional reasoning and planning capabilities. This simplicity enables straightforward comparison across models while ensuring that the evaluation remains rigorous and meaningful.
