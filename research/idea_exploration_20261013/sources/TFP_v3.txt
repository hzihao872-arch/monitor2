0112: However, many VLA policies remain largely reactive: each action is predicted
0113: from the current observation, instruction, and proprioceptive state. This is
0114: insufficient for stage-dependent manipulation, where the same visible scene may
0115: require different actions depending on latent task progress and previous
0116: interaction outcomes. For example, in object swapping, a visually similar state
0117: may correspond to moving the first object to a buffer, moving the second object
0118: into the first object’s original location, or terminating the task.
0119: This motivates adding memory, or task belief, to VLA policies. We use
0120: “belief” to denote a learned latent memory state that summarizes hidden task
0121: progress relevant to future actions. However, robotic manipulation requires more than storing past observations. A useful belief must also decide
0122: when
0123: to change. During
0124: stable transport or occlusion, the policy should preserve task-progress
0125: information despite ambiguous visual evidence; near contact, release, or
0126: subgoal completion, it should rapidly incorporate new evidence. Thus, the key
0127: question is not only whether a VLA has memory, but whether its memory update
0128: follows the event structure and temporal dynamics of manipulation.
0129: Existing memory-aware robot policies mainly either retrieve from history buffers
0130: or maintain recurrent latent states

0142: . History-based methods preserve rich episodic
0143: detail but do not explicitly form a compact task-progress state; recurrent
0144: methods provide such a state, but updates are typically indexed by policy updates rather than manipulation events and elapsed
0145: physical time.
0146: We argue that chunked visuomotor control calls for a dynamics-aware belief
0147: update. A practical controller may re-query earlier when a chunk becomes unreliable, unstable, or
0148: requires correction after contact. Thus, the interval between policy queries is
0149: a physical-time variable, not merely a discrete step index.
0150: Liquid Time-Constant (LTC) networks provide a natural mechanism for such belief
0151: tracking. Their hidden states evolve through input-dependent time constants,
0152: allowing the model to retain information when task context should persist and to
0153: update more rapidly when new evidence appears

0161: manipulation, where completed subgoals, prior contacts, and interaction outcomes
0162: may remain relevant across multiple action chunks.
0163: We introduce
0164: Temporally Conditioned Memory-Fusion Policies (TFP)
0165: , a
0166: belief-conditioned VLA framework for robotic manipulation. TFP maintains an
0167: episode-local latent belief with LTC dynamics: the previous hidden state carries
0168: retained task context, the current visual and proprioceptive observation proposes
0169: an update, and elapsed time controls how strongly the belief is retained or
0170: revised. The updated belief is projected into the action-head conditioning space
0171: and injected through AdaLN-style modulation, allowing temporal context to
0172: directly shape the flow-matching action distribution rather than serve only as
0173: passive historical context.
0174: We evaluate TFP on simulation benchmarks and real-world memory-dependent
0175: manipulation tasks. TFP consistently improves over reactive, history-based, and
0176: latent-state baselines. Mechanistic analyses further show that the learned
0177: belief stabilizes task-progress representations and causally modulates generated
0178: action chunks. Our contributions are:
0179: •
0180: We formulate stage-dependent manipulation as dynamics-aware belief
0181: tracking, where the correct action depends not only on latent task progress and
0182: interaction history, but also on when the internal belief should be revised or
0183: preserved.
0184: •
0185: We propose TFP, a belief-conditioned VLA framework that maintains a
0186: continuous-time latent belief using LTC dynamics and injects this belief
0187: directly into flow-matching action generation. We further introduce
0188: Episode-Aware Temporal Batching (EATB) to train recurrent belief states
0189: efficiently while preserving episode-local hidden-state continuity.
0190: •
0191: We show that TFP improves memory-dependent control in simulation and
0192: on a real Galaxea A1 robot, and provide mechanistic analyses showing that
0193: the learned belief stabilizes task-progress representations and causally
0194: modulates generated action chunks.

0258: III
0259: Method
0260: TFP augments a chunked VLA policy with an episode-local latent belief over task
0261: progress and interaction history. At each policy query, the current visual and
0262: proprioceptive observations update this belief through Liquid Time-Constant
0263: (LTC) dynamics, and the updated belief directly conditions the flow-matching
0264: action head through AdaLN-style modulation. This couples memory and action
0265: generation: temporal context is not stored as a passive history buffer, but as a
0266: persistent state that modulates the next generated action chunk.

0802: III-D
0803: Training with Episode-Aware Temporal Batching
0804: Training TFP requires preserving hidden-state continuity within each
0805: demonstration episode. Randomly shuffling individual chunks breaks this
0806: continuity, while full-episode backpropagation is memory-intensive for large VLA
0807: backbones. We therefore introduce

2040: Although LTC and AdaLN add little inference overhead, recurrent full fine-tuning is costly because training must unroll across action chunks to preserve hidden-state continuity and credit assignment. EATB truncates gradients across contiguous chunks, but our largest LIBERO runs still used

2046: VI
2047: Conclusion
2048: We presented Temporally Conditioned Memory-Fusion Policies (TFP), a framework
2049: for extending reactive VLA policies with an explicit task-progress belief for
2050: history-dependent manipulation. TFP maintains this belief with Liquid
2051: Time-Constant dynamics and injects it directly into the flow-matching action
2052: decoder through AdaLN-style conditioning, allowing the learned belief to
2053: shape action generation rather than serve as passive history context. Across
2054: simulation and real-world tasks, TFP improves performance most clearly when the
2055: current observation alone is insufficient, including long-horizon, visually
2056: perturbed, occluded, and stage-dependent manipulation settings. Mechanistic
2057: analyses show that the learned write-gain dynamics change sharply near
2058: manipulation-event boundaries, while hidden-memory interventions show that
2059: changing only the belief can alter the generated action chunk and end-effector
2060: trajectory. These results suggest that memory in VLA policies should act as an
2061: event-sensitive action-conditioning state: retaining task progress during stable
2062: or ambiguous phases, and revising the belief when task-relevant evidence
2063: changes. Future work should combine such dynamics-aware memory with stronger
2064: object-centric grounding, spatial generalization, and compositional planning, while developing more efficient recurrent fine-tuning schemes.

2585: Chunked generative action policies.
2586: Recent imitation policies often predict action chunks rather than single-step actions. ACT learns sequence-level action prediction for fine-grained manipulation, Diffusion Policy represents visuomotor control as conditional denoising over action trajectories, and

TFP addresses this by maintaining a latent belief across policy queries and injecting it directly into the action decoder.
2599: Temporal and continuous-time memory models.
2600: Temporal context can be modeled with recurrent networks, self-attention,state-space models, and continuous-time recurrent dynamics. RNNs, LSTMs, and GRUs provide compact recurrent states for history aggregation, with LSTMs designed for long time-lag dependencies and GRUs offering a gated recurrence

Because each recurrent step may involve a large VLA backbone, an action-generation decoder, and a full action-horizon imitation loss, repeatedly calling the policy across many memory steps can substantially increase training cost and limit temporal parallelism.
2617: Self-attention provides parallel content-based access to history, but using past observations and action chunks as an ever-growing token set increases context length and does not by itself impose a physical-time belief dynamics
2618: [

The resulting memory is then projected into the action-head conditioning space, so temporal belief directly modulates the generated action chunk instead of remaining a passive history representation.
2643: Memory-augmented and history-aware visuomotor policies.
2644: Several recent works augment robot policies with history or memory to address partial observability and long-horizon manipulation. HAMLET adapts pretrained VLAs into history-aware policies by introducing moment tokens and a lightweight memory module that aggregates historical context for action prediction
2645: [
2646: 17
2647: ]
2648: . This provides an efficient alternative to naively appending multiple past frames, but its memory primarily serves as a token-level history compression and retrieval interface. MemoryVLA introduces a cognition-memory-action framework, where perceptual and cognitive tokens form working memory, retrieve decision-relevant entries from a memory bank, and condition a diffusion action expert
2649: [
2650: 27
2651: ]
2652: . AVA-VLA reformulates VLA control from a POMDP perspective and uses a recurrent belief state to actively modulate visual token processing
2653: [
2654: 33
2655: ]
2656: . However, its memory is mainly used to guide perception, whereas the action-generation pathway is not explicitly conditioned by a continuous-time task-progress belief.
2657: History-aware diffusion policies are also related. Causal Diffusion Policy conditions diffusion-based visuomotor control on historical action sequences through a causal transformer, improving temporal coherence and robustness under degraded observations
2658: [
2659: 20
2660: ]
2661: . Its focus is autoregressive action history modeling rather than language-conditioned VLA control, and its own limitation notes that extremely long-horizon tasks requiring sustained planning remain unresolved. These methods show that exposing the policy to better,richer, or more retrievable history is valuable. TFP addresses a complementary problem: the policy should not only access history, but should maintain a compact latent belief whose evolution follows temporal-conditioned task dynamics.
2662: Concretely, TFP compresses history into a fixed-size continuous-time belief state with input-dependent LTC retention and injects this belief directly into the generative action decoder through AdaLN-style modulation. Thus, memory is not only a retrieved context or an auxiliary visual-attention signal; it directly modulates the decoder activations and therefore the flow-matching velocity field over the predicted action chunk. This makes TFP better aligned with real manipulation dynamics, where the correct action depends on how task progress evolves over physical time, not merely on whether the model can attend to a more complete history.

2664: Additional Method Details
2665: B-A
2666: Memory-Conditioning Interface
2667: TFP conditions the action decoder through adaptive modulation rather than
2668: explicit memory-token cross-attention. Cross-attention-based memory injection
2669: requires the decoder to retrieve and interpret a separate set of memory tokens.
2670: In contrast, adaptive conditioning compresses temporal context into a single
2671: modulation vector that directly shapes decoder activations. The timestep
2672: embedding captures where the denoising process is, while the memory condition
2673: captures what temporal context is relevant. Their additive fusion lets the
2674: decoder jointly adapt to diffusion phase and retained task belief without adding
2675: an extra attention pathway.
2676: This choice reflects the role of LTC memory as a compact time-consistent belief
2677: state rather than a retrievable history buffer. We do not claim that
2678: cross-attention is inherently inferior; instead, AdaLN provides a simpler
2679: fixed-size interface for injecting belief into every action-decoder layer. A
2680: computational overhead discussion is provided in

2860: Appendix C
2861: Continuous-Time Belief Interpretation
2862: This section formalizes the connection between Liquid Time-Constant (LTC) memory
2863: and belief filtering in partially observable manipulation. We do not claim that
2864: LTCs are universally more expressive than all recurrent models. A sufficiently
2865: large recurrent network with access to elapsed time may approximate many
2866: belief-update functions. Instead, our claim is more specific: LTC provides a
2867: structural inductive bias that matches a class of continuous-time,
2868: multi-timescale belief dynamics. This class is particularly relevant for
2869: chunked robot manipulation, where policy queries may occur after variable
2870: execution intervals.
2871: Claim.
2872: For manipulation tasks that can be modeled as partially observable decision
2873: processes with latent task state, the desired memory should behave as a
2874: time-consistent belief state. LTC implements the exact discretization of a
2875: first-order continuous-time belief relaxation process, and its exponential
2876: retention gate is the unique continuous scalar retention rule satisfying
2877: elapsed-time consistency. Step-indexed recurrent baselines such as GRUs or
2878: LSTMs do not enforce this property unless elapsed time is explicitly provided
2879: and the gate is constrained to satisfy the same exponential semigroup structure.

4394: different structure. The decisive variable is a discrete hidden object-location
4395: binding: which visually identical mug hides the ball. Once the ball is occluded,
4396: the current observation provides little corrective evidence about this latent
4397: identity-location association. The policy must therefore write the correct
4398: binding early and retrieve it at the final touch decision. This is closer to
4399: object-centric episodic memory or key-value binding than to continuously
4400: evolving manipulation-stage dynamics.
4401: Sparse opportunities for belief correction.
4402: In many manipulation tasks, the belief can be refined as new contact, motion,
4403: object-state, or subgoal-completion evidence appears. For example, after a
4404: grasp, release, or placement event, the observation gives new evidence about
4405: task progress, and an event-sensitive memory update can revise the internal
4406: state accordingly. In ShellGameTouch, by contrast, the crucial information is
4407: available only before and during occlusion. After the mugs become visually
4408: ambiguous, later observations mainly support execution rather than correction
4409: of the hidden ball-location belief. As a result, a weak or incorrect initial
4410: memory write is difficult to repair through subsequent LTC updates.
4411: Representation mismatch.
4412: This failure mode can also be viewed through a representation-learning lens.
4413: Prior work on interpreting neural robot policies argues that useful policy
4414: representations often align with task-relevant factors of variation, such as
4415: skills, behaviors, or strategies
4416: [
4417: 32
4418: ]
4419: . Under this view,
4420: ShellGameTouch stresses a different dominant factor of variation from the one
4421: most directly targeted by TFP. The task-relevant factor in ShellGameTouch is a
4422: persistent categorical object-location variable, whereas the factor targeted by
4423: TFP is event-sensitive task-progress dynamics. TFP can still improve
4424: ShellGameTouch because its belief state preserves useful latent context, but
4425: the memory is not explicitly structured as a symbolic object map, slot memory,
4426: or key-value store over hidden cup identities.
4427: Failure categories.
4428: Qualitatively, we observe four main failure modes. First, the policy may fail
4429: to encode the correct ball–mug association before occlusion, especially when
4430: the relevant visual cue is brief or spatially small. Second, the memory may
4431: retain a coarse task-progress signal but not a sufficiently precise
4432: object-location binding, causing the final touch to target the wrong mug.
4433: Third, the retained belief may be correct, but the action decoder may fail to
4434: ground that belief into the correct end-effector target under visually similar
4435: mugs. Finally, some failures are low-level execution errors near the final
4436: target rather than clear memory errors. These categories suggest that
4437: ShellGameTouch requires both persistent memory and precise object-centric
4438: grounding.
4439: Interpretation.
4440: We therefore treat ShellGameTouch as a same-backbone diagnostic rather than a
4441: direct state-of-the-art comparison. The improvement over the reactive
4442: baselines shows that TFP provides useful memory under occlusion, but
4443: the absolute success rate reflects the mismatch between ShellGameTouch’s
4444: categorical hidden-location memory and TFP’s primary design target:
4445: continuous-time, event-sensitive manipulation belief dynamics. A natural future
4446: direction is to combine TFP with an object-centric memory module, such as a
4447: slot-based belief map or a retrieval mechanism for discrete hidden-state
4448: bindings. Such a hybrid would allow the policy to retain symbolic
4449: object-location facts while still using LTC dynamics to update task-progress
4450: belief around manipulation events.
