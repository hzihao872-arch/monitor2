Abstract
Learned visual reward models are increasingly used to optimize robot policies,
yet a reward model can score an execution that acts on the wrong object as
highly as one that completes the task. We show that optimizing such a reward
can amplify these wrong-object failures while reward and task success both
rise, so the signals a practitioner would normally monitor look healthy.
We fine-tune every denoiser parameter of a diffusion policy against Robometer
on a drawer task. Starting from a supervised policy with no prior reward
exposure, five training runs raise task success by 10.2 percentage points and
wrong-object failures by 10.9 points on 512 evaluation seeds, whereas five runs
trained on the simulator’s task-completion signal raise success without
amplifying wrong-object failures (difference 9.2 points, 95% CI 5.6 to 13.0).
The amplification recurs from a policy previously optimized against learned
rewards, under the policy’s native diffusion sampler, at matched distance from
the initial policy, and across constrained-policy experiments with two critics
and two optimizers. A tilt model explains when it occurs: under KL-regularized
optimization, an outcome becomes more frequent whenever its expected reward
under the initial policy exceeds the population average. Robometer separates
successes from failures well overall (AUROC .81) but scores wrong-object
failures slightly above successes (AUROC .37), so optimization raises both.
The same model predicts the outcome shifts across 26 constrained settings
(Spearman .89), including those in which task success falls, and Robometer’s
own published success-termination recipe inherits the error. A frozen outcome
verifier redirects the same optimization toward the requested task.

Introduction
Learned visual reward models provide feedback for robot policy optimization
from camera observations and a task description, replacing hand-designed task
rewards
(
Lee et al., 2026
;
Liang et al., 2026
)
. A reward model does not
need to fail broadly to steer a policy wrong. It can score most failures
correctly and still reward one coherent semantic mistake, and optimization is
built to find exactly such mistakes.
Consider a robot instructed to open the middle drawer
(Figure
1
). Opening the upper drawer instead looks like
manipulation progress but fails the requested task. A stacking policy can
likewise build a stable stack with the wrong cube. We call these outcomes
wrong-object failures
: executions that manipulate a distractor in a
task-consistent way while the requested task fails.
Liang et al. (2026)
showed that RoboReward assigns high scores to such executions and that these
false positives reinforce incorrect behavior during online learning. What is
less understood is how such errors interact with genuine task learning. If
optimization simply degraded task success, standard evaluation would catch it.
We show that it does not have to: optimizing a visual reward can raise task
success and wrong-object failures at the same time. The two checks a
practitioner relies on, the reward model’s aggregate success–failure
discrimination and the policy’s task success after optimization, can both
look healthy while a specific semantic failure grows.

Why does a reward with strong aggregate discrimination produce this? A
trajectory-level audit shows that Robometer separates successes from all
failures well (AUROC .813) but scores wrong-object failures slightly above
successes (AUROC .372); good discrimination against the far more common other
failures hides the inversion. A simple model turns this observation into a
prediction. Under KL-regularized optimization the optimal policy tilts the
initial policy toward high reward, and an outcome becomes more frequent exactly
when its average tilted reward exceeds the population average. A reward that
scores wrong-object failures about as highly as successes therefore raises
both, and stronger optimization is decided by the upper tail of the score
distribution. Fitted with a single strength parameter, the model reproduces
the outcome changes across 26 constrained settings (Spearman .89 for
wrong-object failures and .79 for success), including the drawer settings in
which task success falls, and it places the full-denoiser and constrained
results on one continuum of optimization strength. Robometer’s own published
recipe, dense progress with success-triggered termination, does not escape the
error: its success detector fires on 89% of wrong-object executions and on
73% of successes.
Finally, we use a frozen success verifier trained on outcome labels as a
diagnostic intervention. Reweighting the critic reward by the verifier raises
task success by 7.1 points and lowers wrong-object failures by 4.4 points
relative to critic-only training, and verifier-only rewards match it, which
identifies outcome supervision as the source of the recovery.
Contributions.
(i) We show, with independent training runs and simulator-measured outcomes,
that optimizing a visual reward can amplify wrong-object failures while task
success rises, and that a ground-truth completion reward under the same
optimizer does not. (ii) We show that strong aggregate discrimination can hide
the semantic misranking that drives this amplification, and give a tilt model
that predicts the resulting outcome changes across settings and unifies
semantic amplification with classical success loss. (iii) We show that outcome
verification redirects the same optimization toward the intended task.
Scope.
The simulated experiments cover occluded-drawer manipulation and
identity-sensitive stacking. Full-denoiser fine-tuning uses Robometer’s
terminal success probability on two drawer layouts; constrained experiments
add LIV and stacking. The verifier is task-specific and trained on simulator
labels. The real-robot study is a case study with one training run per
fine-tuned condition.

Related Work
Reward overoptimization and reward-model evaluation.
Learned rewards turn human preferences into policy objectives
(
Christiano et al., 2017
)
, but optimizing such a proxy can depart
from the intended outcome
(
Amodei et al., 2016
;
Skalse et al., 2022
)
,
and more capable agents exploit misspecified rewards more effectively
(
Pan et al., 2022
)
. Overoptimization has been characterized in
language-model and diffusion-model alignment
(
Gao et al., 2023
;
Zhang et al., 2024
)
, where it
appears as a decline in a gold or held-out metric under continued proxy
optimization. We study a signature this diagnostic misses: task success
rises while a specific semantic failure grows, both measured through
simulator state independently of the reward. Under a KL penalty the optimal
policy is an exponential tilt of the reference policy
(
Korbak et al., 2022
;
Rafailov et al., 2023
)
; we use this closed form to predict
which outcomes optimization amplifies and to place semantic amplification and
the classical decline of the gold metric on one continuum. Reward-model evaluation raises
a related concern. Low prediction error need not imply low regret after
optimization
(
Fluri et al., 2025
)
, and static ranking accuracy
imperfectly predicts post-optimization behavior
(
Kim et al., 2025b
;
Wen et al., 2025
)
. Aggregate metrics can also
conceal poor performance on meaningful subsets, a phenomenon known as hidden
stratification
(
Oakden-Rayner et al., 2020
)
. Section
5.3
shows such masking in a visual reward model, on the very subset that
optimization amplifies.
Visual rewards for robot learning.
Visual rewards have been learned from human videos
(
Chen et al., 2021
)
,
goal-conditioned value pretraining
(
Ma et al., 2023b
)
and language–image
alignment
(
Ma et al., 2023a
)
. RoboCLIP and vision–language model reward
models (VLM-RMs) use pretrained video– and image–language models as
rewards
(
Sontakke et al., 2023
;
Rocamonde et al., 2024
)
, and Video-Language
Critic learns video–text alignment and temporal rankings
(
Alakuijala et al., 2025
)
. RoboReward and Robometer scale general-purpose
robotic reward models
(
Lee et al., 2026
;
Liang et al., 2026
)
. Our tasks
hinge on spatial reference and color-based object identity, distinctions that
match documented weaknesses of vision–language models
(
Kamath et al., 2023
;
Yuksekgonul et al., 2023
)
.
Liang et al. (2026)
document RoboReward false positives on
wrong-object executions and their consequences for online reinforcement
learning (RL). We find the same failure class in Robometer’s own success
output, where strong aggregate discrimination hides it, and show that
optimizing against it can raise task success and wrong-object failures
together. Robometer’s published online RL pairs dense progress rewards with
success-triggered termination; on our drawer task that success detector fires
on more wrong-object executions than successes, so the recipe inherits the
same error (Appendix
I
).
Outcome supervision and reward correction.
Success classifiers have long served as rewards, from event-based control
(
Fu et al., 2018
)
to vision–language success detectors
(
Du et al., 2023
)
, and outcome verifiers trained on correctness
labels are widely used in language-model reasoning
(
Cobbe et al., 2021
)
.
Other work corrects the reward itself: ensembles support conservative
optimization against uncertain feedback
(
Coste et al., 2024
)
,
Adapt2Reward uses failure prompts to adapt visual rewards
(
Yang et al., 2024
)
, and RARM uses reference-anchored,
confidence-gated progress matching
(
Yang et al., 2026
)
. Our verifier is a
task-specific success classifier trained on simulator labels; we use it as a
diagnostic that tests whether changing the feedback redirects the amplified
failure.
Diffusion-policy optimization.
Diffusion Policy (DP)
(
Chi et al., 2023
)
generates robot action
chunks by iterative denoising. Treating the denoising chain as a Markov
decision process (MDP) enables reward-driven fine-tuning of the full denoiser
(
Black et al., 2024
;
Ren et al., 2025
)
, while latent-noise steering improves a
fixed policy by optimizing the noise supplied to its denoiser
(
Wagenmaker et al., 2025
)
. Our two protocols follow these routes:
full-denoiser PPO under the denoising-MDP formulation, and a constrained
selector over a fixed bank of noise sequences with the decoder frozen.
Generalist vision–language–action (VLA) policies such as OpenVLA
(
Kim et al., 2025a
)
can also be adapted with RL
(
Chen et al., 2025
)
; we
use task-specific DPs, which let us evaluate many independent training runs
against simulator-measured outcomes.

4.2
Constrained Closed-loop Noise Policies
The constrained experiments test whether the pattern recurs across drawer
manipulation and identity-sensitive stacking, using Robometer and LIV under
PPO and grouped GRPO. Within each setting, reward objectives share a frozen
diffusion decoder and a fixed bank of 24 noise sequences. A trainable
categorical policy selects one sequence per episode, and the decoder
generates actions from current observations throughout execution.
The drawer selector learns unconditional probabilities; the stacking
selector conditions on the initial visual observation.
Both optimizers update the selector probabilities from executed rollouts.
Grouped GRPO
(
Shao et al., 2024
)
standardizes rewards within groups of four
executions that share the initial state and images. Training starts from
the uniform selector and uses eight rounds: 192 feedback episodes per
objective for PPO and 256 for GRPO. Evaluation uses the final eighth
checkpoint on 32 new seeds for drawer Robometer PPO and 64 per task/color
for the other panels. Repetitions vary training and evaluation seeds on
the same noise support; Appendix
D
gives
replication and optimizer details.
4.3
Evaluation and Uncertainty
Every full-denoiser comparison is the fixed iteration-40 policy against its
study’s shared initialization on that study’s 512 evaluation seeds, with one
renderer, evaluator and scorer for all policies. The same seeds evaluate
iterations 10, 20 and 30 of the warm-start critic and Product runs, which
describe training dynamics. We report changes in success and wrong-object
failure in percentage points (pp) with 95% bootstrap confidence intervals
(CIs). Per-training intervals resample paired evaluation seeds. Pooled
intervals resample training runs and one shared draw of evaluation seeds per
replicate; contrasts between rewards resample the two families independently.
Constrained-policy intervals condition on the fitted policies and fixed noise
supports. Appendix
B.2
gives per-training results and
alternative resampling designs, and Appendix
F
the
constrained grid.

9
Discussion and Limitations
Evaluate rewards by what optimization will amplify.
A rising success rate can hide a growing tendency to act on the wrong object,
and a strong aggregate AUROC can hide the misranking that causes it. The tilt
model makes the relevant quantity explicit: optimization amplifies every
outcome whose reward sits above the population average under the current
policy, and strong optimization is decided by the top of the score
distribution. Reward models should therefore be evaluated on how they score
semantically coherent failures relative to successes and on what occupies
their upper tail, measured on the behaviors that optimization reaches, and
policy optimization should be monitored with outcome measurements that are
independent of the reward.
Limitations.
Our simulated experiments cover two tasks; full-denoiser fine-tuning uses one
critic on two drawer layouts, with five runs per objective, and the supervised
layout was selected by a fixed capability screen. For the full denoiser the
tilt model is a first-order description, because fine-tuning creates behaviors
that reweighting the initial policy cannot represent; for the constrained
selector it is the exact optimum up to estimation. The verifier is
task-specific and trained on simulator labels, so it diagnoses rather than
replaces learned visual rewards. Product results are reported under the
fine-tuning sampler. A zero critic score remains zero after multiplication, and
because a wrong-object failure requires task failure, a lower wrong-object rate
alone does not imply fewer distractor actions. The hardware study is a case
study with one training run per fine-tuned condition.
10
Conclusion
CriticHack
shows that optimizing a learned visual reward can improve task success
while amplifying wrong-object failures, that a ground-truth completion reward
under the same optimizer does not, and that the effect survives a change of
initialization and sampler. A tilt model explains when it happens: an outcome
grows whenever the reward scores it above the population average, so a reward
that scores semantic errors about as highly as successes raises both, and
when semantic errors hold the top of the score distribution, stronger
optimization turns this into the classical loss of task success.
Aggregate reward-model metrics hide this structure, and outcome verification
redirects the same optimization toward the intended task. Learned rewards
should be evaluated through the behaviors they reinforce.

B.4
Policy, scorer and evaluation provenance
Initial policy.
The shared initial weights are not a purely
supervised policy. They descend from a diffusion policy trained by supervised
learning on middle-drawer demonstrations, followed by three earlier updates
in this project: (i) a five-epoch warm start on the supervised data mixed with
copies of one optimized episode from an earlier critic study (5.3% of
samples; that episode pushes the distractor drawer closed without opening the
target, which is not the wrong-object pattern measured here); (ii) online RL
against RoboReward-8B; and (iii) 600 steps of offline reward-weighted
denoising regression toward Robometer on the evaluation scene, which in its
own evaluation raised wrong-object failures from 1 to 7 of 32. The
whole-network study therefore starts from a policy already exposed to the
critic it optimizes. Every reported change is measured against this common
initialization, and the constrained-selector experiments use the same
decoder.
Scorer.
Robometer-4B at a fixed revision scores eight evenly
spaced frames of a third-person camera with the task text “open the middle
drawer”; the reward is the success probability at the last frame, and the
base vision-language model is loaded offline at a fixed revision. The
verifier is the frozen model of Section
7
. Rescoring 32
stored episodes on a second installation reproduced the stored Robometer
scores, all eight per-frame success probabilities and the verifier outputs
exactly (maximum absolute difference 0).
All primary critic and product evaluations use the same OSMesa renderer,
evaluator and scorer. Weights are fixed at iteration 40; no intermediate
checkpoint is selected by its evaluation outcome. The training sampler is
stochastic DDIM with five steps and standard deviation .05. The artifact
records model identities, evaluation seeds, configurations and score files.

Figure 10:

The main hardware outcomes are reported in

Physical setup and reset procedure.
Experiments use a Franka Research 3 robot in a physical drawer setup
matched to the simulation at 1:1 scale, including cabinet placement,
drawer geometry, initial drawer openings, and camera placement.
The requested task is to open the middle drawer. Before each trial,
the target middle drawer is reset to its closed position, and the
upper distractor drawer is returned to the same marked partial
opening. All policies are evaluated under the same physical setup
and deployment configuration.
Policy identities and checkpoint mapping.
The hardware comparison includes three conditions:
Initialization
,
Critic-trained
and
Completion-trained
. Initialization is the shared warm-start
initialization of the full-denoiser study (Appendix
B.4
),
which has prior reward exposure. Critic-trained is Critic 5 at iteration 40,
chosen after the simulation evaluations without a prespecified quantitative
selection rule; the checkpoint was fixed before any physical trials.
No hardware outcomes were used in the choice.
Among the five critic trainings, Critic 5 has the second-largest
wrong-object increase on the 128-seed checkpoint-diagnostic panel of
Section
5.3
(per-training values in
Appendix
J

Appendix
C.1

used during fine-tuning. Because only one training history per fine-tuned
condition was deployed, the hardware study is a sim-to-real case study
rather than an estimate of transfer across training histories.
Deployment and evaluation.
Each policy executes 40 trials, giving 120 trials across the three
conditions. Policy parameters remain fixed throughout hardware
evaluation, with no training or fine-tuning on the physical robot.
The evaluation therefore examines the physical execution of
policies trained in simulation. Task outcomes are assessed from
physical drawer motion independently of the learned visual reward.

L.4
Ceiling-limited supervised initialization
To test for amplification from a reward-naive policy, we declared, before
any launch, three Robometer and three terminal-oracle trainings from a
supervised-only checkpoint of the same diffusion policy. It was fine-tuned
only by supervised learning on the 350 successful middle-drawer
demonstrations, each rendered with and without a visual occluder, with no
critic and no reinforcement learning. It is not a one-factor removal of
critic exposure: its observation encoder and normalizer differ from the
shared warm start, so it is run with its own architecture checkpoint, and
frozen-verifier scores on its features are not interpreted. All other
settings are copied from critic 3, and the endpoint protocol is unchanged.

M.2
Endpoint Image Interventions and Actual Verifier Inputs
The R187 image diagnostic retains all 64 final original-LIV executions.
Recorded actions reproduce initial states and all 19,200 joint steps
exactly; captured preintegration poses recover every original terminal
image pixel for pixel. Six terminal variants per execution keep the
earlier seven frames, other joints and frozen float32 LIV scorer fixed.
Unchanged native scores reproduce with maximum error zero. Setting
target opening to 120 mm lowers native reward in all 64 states, by
.003560–.016642. Restoring the upper drawer to its initial 57 mm
opening lowers reward in 63/64. The target and upper effects interact:
partial target progress has different reward effects at observed and
initial upper positions. This is an image-scoring intervention, not
an additional executed policy, a reachable action counterfactual, or
identification of a training-time causal effect.
Verifier recomputation.
The verifier receives recorded policy-camera embeddings, not these
critic-camera images. Recomputing its actual late-five-plus-initial
input on all 960 R182/R183/R187 endpoint traces exactly reproduces
every verifier and product score. Among 53 R187 matched seeds where
oracle succeeds and LIV fails, original LIV misranks 52 pairs; verifier
and product both correct 50 of those and retain two errors. Across all
ten arm pairs within each of the three trainings, their ranking signs
agree on every success-discordant pair. This locates the observed
binary-ranking correction in the verifier, without establishing an
extra original-LIV contribution or identical downstream policy updates.
M.3
Confidence-floor Intervention
Under GRPO, a trajectory with small positive reward can have positive
group-relative advantage if its peers score lower. With nonnegative group
rewards, zero reward has nonpositive advantage. A fixed confidence-floor
intervention in R176 sets products with verifier probability below the
existing
.5
.5
threshold to zero, retaining matched reference conditions
and the same head without a threshold sweep.
All 480 training and 384 final executions complete. Distinct-color Robometer
wrong-object failures decrease from 12 to seven/64 relative to the old
product, with a reliable interval; success changes from 52 to 54 with
an interval crossing zero. Near-color no-stack failures increase.
The corresponding complete LIV R177 intervention does not establish an
increment over its original product. No new labels are added, and neither
experiment is a new independent training repetition of the main method.

fit on 64 controls and evaluate on 64 additional executions from 16 new
perturbed starts, containing 26 successes and 38 failures. Both full-history
and last-quarter heads classify all 64 correctly. A subsequent matched
table-camera-only ablation, reusing these controls, preserves perfect
classification. The terminal images therefore contain usable outcome
evidence; history’s necessity is not established.
Sixteen of the 26 successes receive exactly zero from last-quarter
RoboReward. Multiplication cannot make those scores positive, and retains
the 40.625% strict failure-preference rate, including 22 reversed
within-start pairs. Neither critic assigns a failure a high score in
either window. These are executed physical controls and static reward
tests, without learned-policy hacking induction or demonstrated RL
mitigation.
